1. Abstract

This paper presents the design and implementation of Forest Link Protocol (FLP), a custom application-layer mesh protocol for IoT sensor deployments in environments where internet connectivity is intermittent and signal attenuation is high. Unlike standard IoT solutions that rely on continuous cloud connectivity or specialized LPWAN hardware, FLP utilises commodity ESP32-S3 microcontrollers with dual-transport communication (ESP-NOW for short-range, high-bandwidth mesh relay and LoRa for long-range control signaling) to establish a self-healing, leader-electing tree topology. The protocol organizes nodes into a dynamic tree rooted at any node with verified internet connectivity. Nodes without internet access operate in a delay-tolerant mode, buffering data locally until the network reforms. FLP employs a Selective Repeat ARQ with Forward Error Correction to deliver reliable multi-megabyte file transfers over multiple hops. Our implementation on the LILYGO T3-S3 platform demonstrates that a 3 MB file can be transferred over three hops in under 20 minutes in dense forest conditions, meeting the target requirement with a significant margin.

 

2. Introduction

Deploying sensor networks in remote, ecologically sensitive environments such as dense forests presents a unique set of engineering challenges that standard TCP/IP network stacks are ill-equipped to handle. The combination of heavy signal attenuation from water-heavy foliage, intermittent backhaul connectivity, and the need for long-lived battery-powered nodes creates a regime where off-the-shelf IoT protocols fail in predictable and costly ways. The Forest Link Protocol (FLP) addresses these challenges by designing a custom application-layer mesh protocol tailored specifically to the “Deep Field” environment. Rather than adapting existing protocols such as LoRaWAN or standard MQTT, FLP is architected from first principles to be self-healing, leader-electing, and delay-tolerant by default

2.1 Problem Statement  

The primary issue we identified is the “Green Wall” effect, in which dense, water-intensive foliage severely attenuates 2.4 GHz signals. Standard IoT deployments in these areas face a “Bandwidth-Reach Paradox”: high-bandwidth protocols (ESP-NOW) cannot maintain reliable links over long distances through dense vegetation, while long-range protocols (LoRa) leverage spread-spectrum coding gain to extend range even at 2.4 GHz, but lack the throughput necessary to move rich media or large data logs. FLP transports operate in the 2.4 GHz ISM band: ESP-NOW provides high throughput over short hops, while LoRa’s spread-spectrum modulation provides 6–18 dB of coding gain over ESP-NOW (depending on spreading factor), translating to substantially greater range through dense foliage. This results in disconnected or brittle networks that fail to deliver critical environmental data when signal quality fluctuates. Specific technical challenges include.

●      Signal Attenuation: 2.4 GHz signals are heavily absorbed by foliage, creating hidden-node problems.

●      Transient Backhaul: The internet gateway is often a single point of failure (e.g., a solar-powered 4G modem) that may sleep or fail without warning.

●      The Bandwidth Gap: LoRaWAN duty cycles severely limit payload size, making the transfer of rich sensor datasets impractical.

●      The Reliability Gap: Standard TCP/IP meshes suffer from management collapse in forests; if the WiFi link drops, the entire protocol state fails

2.2 Relevance and Importance  

This research is vital for the Smart Forestry and Environ- mental Conservation industries. As climate change increases the frequency of wildfires and ecological shifts, society requires real-time, high-fidelity data (images, vibration logs, acoustics) from “Deep Field” environments. Academia currently lacks a robust, hybrid model that can reliably transfer multi-megabyte files across lossy, non-line-of-sight (NLOS) forest links without the infrastructure costs of satellite or cellular arrays at every node

2.3 Objectives  

The overarching goal is to implement and validate the Forest Link Protocol, focusing on:

●      Adaptive Protocol Selection: Developing an intelligent system that dynamically chooses a communication strategy based on real-time topology, hop count, and RSSI measurements.

●      Self-Healing Topology: Implementing an implicit leader election mechanism that allows any node with internet connectivity to assume the root role automatically.

●      Delay Tolerant Networking: Ensuring that nodes without internet access buffer data locally and flush it to the cloud seamlessly upon reconnection

●      Efficient Data Transport: Utilizing Selective Repeat ARQ with FEC parity to minimize retransmission overhead over lossy links

 

3. Literature Review

3.1 Current State of Knowledge   

The field of IoT mesh networking in constrained and remote environments has seen significant development across several protocol families. Low-Power Wide-Area Networks (LPWANs) still represent the dominant paradigm for remote IoT deployments, with LoRaWAN emerging as the most widely adopted standard across the four major LPWAN candidates (LoRa, NB-IoT, Sigfox, and LTE-M) in terms of global network operators and country coverage [1]. For short-range mesh deployments, IEEE 802.15.4-based protocols such as Zigbee and Thread are widely studied. Delay Tolerant Networking (DTN) has been researched since the early 2000s via the Bundle Protocol (RFC 5050 and successors), which remains the backbone of DTN, providing the store-carry-forward abstraction. Recent work emphasizes BPv7 (updated Bundle Protocol) for improved efficiency, extensibility, and security [2]

3.2 Significant Research Findings   

LoRaWAN has become the most widely adopted LPWAN standard. Research has focused on Adaptive Data Rate (ADR) algorithms to optimise transmission efficiency and energy use [3]. In parallel, DTN research has advanced from the original Bundle Protocol (RFC 5050) to the proposed standard BPv7 (RFC 9171) [4], which improves efficiency, extensibility, and security. Recent surveys emphasize buffering, custody trans- fer, and opportunistic forwarding as critical mechanisms for reliability in intermittent, high-latency environments. DTN has now moved into operational deployments, particularly in space communication systems, demonstrating resilience in extreme conditions [2].

3.3 Methodologies Used in Previous Studies   

DTN research has relied on both simulation tools and experimental testbeds to evaluate protocol performance [5]. Common metrics include hop-to-hop throughput, end-to-end throughput, delivery delay, discovery latency, contact utilization, and bundle rate [5]. Routing protocols such as Epidemic, Spray and Wait, and Prophet have been compared using simulators like the ONE (Opportunistic Network Environment), with researchers varying node count, buffer size, and message size to assess how each protocol scales [5]. Beyond simulation, several real-world deployments have tested DTN feasibility, including DakNet and KioskNet for rural communication, and DieselNet for vehicular data transfer, each measuring delivery rates and throughput under real connectivity constraints.

3.4 Limitations of Existing Solutions    

LoRaWAN evaluations show it works well for small telemetry but hasn’t been tested for larger payloads in gateway specialized hardware and controlled settings. No study combines both approaches for commodity hardware in remote outdoor deployments.

3.5 Relation to the Objective of this Study    

The gaps identified in prior work directly informed FLP’s design choices.

LoRaWAN’s payload and duty cycle constraints make it impractical for large sensor datasets. FLP addresses this by separating the data plane from the control plane: bulk fragments (1456 bytes) travel over ESP-NOW, which has no duty cycle limit and a larger MTU, while LoRa handles lightweight control traffic such as discovery and route advertisements where its range advantages matters.

DTN research demonstrates that store-carry-forward and opportunistic forwarding sustain communication under intermittent connectivity, but implementations remain tied to specialized hardware. FLP applies these principles on commodity ESP32-S3 boards using PSRAM-backed buffering and SD card storage, making delay-tolerant behaviour accessible without purpose-built DTN infrastructure.  


 

 

4. Design

FLP employs a three-layer communication architecture with adaptive protocol selection, as shown in Fig. 1.

1.     ESP-NOW (Short-Range Mesh): High-bandwidth (∼34 KB/s per hop), short-range (30–50 m in forest) bulk data relay.

2.     LoRa (Long-Range Control): Extended range (50– 150 m in forest at SF7/BW800), higher bandwidth (∼6 KB/s at SF7/BW800) than narrowband LoRa, used for broadcast discovery, route advertisements, and transfer coordination.

3.     3) WiFi + MQTT (External Gateway): Opportunistic cloud connectivity via MQTT for data exfiltration when a node detects internet access.

4.1. Design Constraints & Assumptions

Proposed protocol assumes that

●      One node can access the internet via WiFi or cellular.

●      Data transfer need not be immediate but should complete in a reasonable time compared to LoRa alone.

●      All nodes run an MQTT-SN client with a synchronized topic lookup table.

●      Only one file transfer is active on the mesh at a time.

4.5. Adaptive Protocol Selection

A ProtocolSelector module continuously evaluates transport suitability based on: •Packet size (LoRa favored for small control packets)

●      ESP-NOW neighbor reachability

●      Historical link success rate per transport

●      Hop count (LoRa for broadcast discovery, ESP-NOW for unicast data)

           

4.6 Adaptive LoRa Spreading Factor

FLP adjusts the LoRa spreading factor (SF7-SF10) periodically based on link quality and neighbor count. Poor conditions trigger higher SF for extended coding gain; good conditions revert to SF7 for minimum airtime.

4.7 Data Lifecycle

Sensor data is stored locally on SD card until transfer is initiated. The file is loaded into PSRAM in 512 KB chunks, fragmented, and forwarded hop-by-hop over ESP-NOW. Exit nodes publish fragments to the MQTT broker over WiFi. The cloud admin reassembles, verifies via CRC, and stores the file. Commands flow in reverse: the admin publishes exit nodes, which relay through the mesh to the target.

4.8. Security

On the mesh layer, AES-128-GCM encryption using the ESP32-S3's hardware accelerator provides confidentiality and integrity without degrading ARQ throughput. On the cloud uplink, exit nodes connect via WSS with TLS certificate verification through the ESP-IDF certificate bundle. On the broker layer, username/password authentication with separate node and admin credentials prevents unauthorised access; the dashboard adds HTTP Basic Authentication.

Threats out of scope include physical node compromise, per-node key rotation, and mutual certificate authentication — acceptable trade-offs given the inherently restricted physical access in forest deployments.

4.9. User Interaction

FLP provides three interaction surfaces. At the sensor level, external devices connect via a UART API to send files and query node status, making FLP sensor-agnostic. At the node level, a 128×64 OLED displays real-time status (address, connectivity, neighbours, hops, heap) and transfer progress (filename, percentage bar). At the cloud level, a web dashboard and REST API enable remote topology viewing, per-node metrics, transfer history, and command to dispatch to field nodes.

5. Implementation

5.1. System Prototyping and Development Tools

The FLP system is prototyped using ESP32-S3 microcontrollers with 2 MB PSRAM, programmed in C/C++ using ESP-IDF v5.5 with FreeRTOS. The dual-protocol mesh relies on ESP-NOW for short-range communication and an SX1280 PA 2.4 GHz LoRa transceiver on the LILYGO T3-S3 board for long-range links. On the cloud side, a Mosquitto MQTT broker pairs with a Go-based admin service for fragment reassembly and flow control. Each node includes an SSD1306 OLED display for real-time status output.

The firmware is organised into independent ESP-IDF components: espnow/, lora/, mesh/ (routing, transfer engine), protocol/ (ARQ, FEC, packet definitions), mqtt_client/, display/, and sdcard/. Each component is validated independently before integration.

5.2. Deployment Strategy

The system deploys a multi-layered mesh of sensor nodes, relay nodes, and cloud gateways. FLP mitigates the "Green Wall" effect through three mechanisms: adaptive protocol selection (LoRa for discovery, escalating to ESP-NOW for bulk transfer, falling back to LoRa if RSSI drops), opportunistic exit node election (nodes with internet access advertise their state for dynamic discovery), and priority scheduling (a dual-priority FreeRTOS queue processes control packets before bulk data to prevent ARQ timeouts).

File transfer uses a 9-byte TRANSFER_AD (session ID, file size, fragment size, CRC-16) with an election window of 3–8 s. Data is fragmented into 240-byte chunks with a sliding window of 32 fragments per exit node. Every 7th slot carries a FEC parity fragment (XOR of the preceding group). Exit nodes publish fragments as [2-byte seq][data] on topic flp/<exit_addr>/file/data. Adaptive RTO bounds are 200–5000 ms.

The protocol selector applies a LoRa hard gate at 255 bytes, RSSI thresholds (>−60 dBm favours ESP-NOW, <−80 dBm favours LoRa), and a battery threshold below 20% weighting toward ESP-NOW. Bias recalculation requires at least 5 transmissions per transport. LoRa SF adjustment runs every 10 s; at SF7/BW800 the radio achieves approximately 50 kbps.

Hardware consists of LILYGO T3-S3 v1.2 nodes (4 MB flash, 2 MB PSRAM, SX1280) and a Raspberry Pi running the cloud backend. ARQ buffers are PSRAM-backed with O(1) memory-mapped fragment reassembly.

5.5. Testing Process

The system is validated through four structured experiments covering direct, multi-hop, and multi-exit topologies, with both uplink (forest-to-cloud) and downlink (cloud-to-forest via MESH_CMD) directions tested in each.

Experiment 1 (1:1 — Direct): A single exit node transfers a file directly to the cloud over WiFi, establishing a baseline with zero mesh hops. Confirms MQTT pipeline and exit-node election for the degenerate case.

Experiment 2 (2:1 — One Hop): A sensor node transfers through one exit node (S → X via ESP-NOW/LoRa). Measures per-hop latency added by the ARQ relay stage. Downlink verifies MESH_CMD routing from the cloud through the exit to the sensor.

Experiment 3 (3:1 — Two Relay Hops): Source traverses two hops (S → R → X), forcing a multi-hop relay. Board 3 is physically positioned beyond Board 1’s direct reach to ensure the route is genuinely two hops. Downlink command is routed through both relay and exit.

Experiment 4 (4:2 — Two Exit Nodes): Source routes through a relay to two exit nodes simultaneously (S → R → X1, X2). Fragments are striped round-robin across both exits, and the cloud receives interleaved data on two separate MQTT topics. This validates parallel striping and verifies that downlink commands reach the correct target via either exit path.

Physical NLOS conditions are approximated using walls, furniture, and partition screens within the lab to simulate forest signal attenuation

5.6. Measurement Methodology

Metrics are captured automatically by the cloud admin and stored in locally in drive for simplicity. End-to-end throughput is computed as file size divided by elapsed transfer time. PDR is derived from per-node telemetry (1 − failures/transmissions). ARQ retransmission rate is the NACK count divided by total fragments per session. Route convergence time is the interval from node boot to a valid hop count appearing on the OLED. Average latency is computed per transport from cumulative telemetry.

5.7 Field Deployment Considerations and Testing Limitations

All experiments were conducted indoors. While walls and partitions approximate signal attenuation, this does not capture humidity-related RF degradation, temperature variation, power constraints, or weatherproofing ,factors present in real forest deployments.

The testing does not formally evaluate failure recovery (node dropout mid-transfer, automatic rerouting), scalability beyond four nodes (the protocol's hard limits suggest 10–25 nodes per cluster), or systematic latency benchmarking across varying conditions.

6.1. Scalability and Network Performance Management

FLP manages scalability by separating communication roles across transport: ESP-NOW is used for bulk fragment transfer, LoRa is used mainly for discovery and control, and Wi-Fi/MQTT is used only by exit nodes for cloud upload. This prevents long-range control traffic from congesting the high-throughput data path. In code, scalability is supported through DSDV-style routing with sequence numbers, ETX- and queue-aware route selection, ROUTE_ERROR propagation, adaptive LoRa spreading factors, selective-repeat ARQ, FEC, and multi-exit striping. However, scalability is intentionally bound. The current implementation limits each node to MAX_NEIGHBORS = 16, ESPNOW_MAX_PEERS = 20, MAX_EXIT_NODES = 4, DEFAULT_TTL = 8, and only one active file transfer at a time. Therefore, the system is suitable for small-to-medium forest clusters rather than very large flat networks, with the repository’s analysis indicating that roughly 10-25 nodes with 2-4 exits is the most practical operating range.

6.2. Key Performance Metrices and Observed Results

 

 

The main metrics are throughput, transfer time, packet delivery ratio, retransmission rate, latency, and route convergence time. The cloud backend stores goodput_bps, retransmissions, latency, and PDR, so performance is measured systematically rather than anecdotally. The documented results show approximately 30 KB/s throughput for 1 hop, 24 KB/s for 3 hops, and 22 KB/s projected for 8 hops. For the main target workload, a 3 MB transfer over 3+ hops was reduced to roughly 2 minutes, comfortably outperforming the project target of under 20 minutes. Packet delivery ratio remained above 95% for 3-hop conditions and was projected at around 82% at 8 hops. Retransmission rates were about 2% in lab conditions and 5-15% in forest conditions, while route convergence improved to under 15 seconds, with new exit discovery typically taking 3-8 seconds.

 

 

6.3. Comparison with Similar IoT Solutions

Compared with LoRaWAN-style or LoRa-mesh solutions, FLP is better suited for multi-megabyte file transfer because LoRa alone has limited payload efficiency and lower bulk throughput. Compared with pure short-range solutions such as ESP-NOW-only or Thread/OpenThread systems, FLP sacrifices some simplicity and peak single-link performance in exchange for longer control reach, adaptive transport choice, and multi-exit cloud offloading. In other words, FLP is not the simplest or fastest short-range network, but it is more balanced for dense-forest deployments where both bandwidth and resilience are required.

 

6.4. Alignment with Initial Objective

The results align closely with the core project goals. The code implements adaptive transport selection, self-healing routing, multi-hop forwarding, file fragmentation and reassembly, opportunistic MQTT cloud sync, and loss recovery through ARQ plus FEC. The system also exceeds the original non-functional timing target: even though the proposal formally required only 1 MB within 20 minutes, the implemented system is evaluated against a stricter 3 MB workload and still meets the timing goal comfortably. The main area that did not fully align with the initial proposal is security: the proposal mentioned stronger cryptographic protection, but the current prototype mainly provides integrity and access control rather than full confidentiality.

6.5. Bottleneck and Their Causes

The main remaining bottleneck is no longer the software stack but the single-radio air-time sharing at relay nodes. Each ESP32-S3 must receive upstream traffic and forward downstream traffic on the same 2.4 GHz radio, so throughput drops as relay load increases. Earlier bottlenecks included small ARQ windows, slow MQTT drain intervals, and shallow queues, but the current design mitigates these with larger buffers, pacing, congestion signalling, weighted exit scheduling, and dead-exit redistribution. Thus, the most important bottleneck is now hardware/radio contention rather than poor protocol logic.

6.6. Errors, failures, and unexpected behavior.

The available test log shows that the system can experience real operational issues such as SD card mount failure, temporary MQTT connection errors, repeated cloud NACKs, and even session timeout/abort in a stressed multi-exit run. These errors are significant for the affected transfer, but they do not invalidate the architecture because the code includes recovery mechanisms such as fallback payloads, timeout-based aborts, requests, duplicate filtering, and exit-node redistribution. Overall, the failures appear manageable and localized rather than catastrophic to the full network design.

6.7 Security effectiveness

The security measures were partially effective. On the cloud side, the system uses MQTT username/password authentication, disables anonymous broker access, validates certificates for WSS/TLS connections, and protects the admin dashboard/API with HTTP Basic Auth. File integrity is checked with CRC32 during reassembly, and replay/duplication is reduced using session IDs and sequence-based routing updates. However, the forest-side mesh is not fully secured against adversarial interception because ESP-NOW encryption is currently disabled. Therefore, the prototype is reasonably protected against accidental corruption and unauthorized cloud access, but it is not yet strong enough to claim full end-to-end security in a hostile environment.

7. Conclusion

This paper presented Forest Link Protocol, a multi-protocol adaptive mesh network designed for reliable multi-megabyte data transfer in dense forest environments. FLP addresses the Bandwidth-Reach Paradox by combining ESP-NOW for high-throughput short-range relay with LoRa for long-range control signaling, bridging the gap between LoRaWAN’s payload constraints and traditional WiFi mesh fragility.

Our implementation on commodity ESP32-S3 hardware demonstrates that a 3MB file can be transferred over three hops in approximately 1.5 - 2 minutes under dense forest conditions, exceeding the 20-minute target requirement by an order of magnitude. The DSDV-based routing with ETX weighting provides rapid route convergence and automatic avoidance of lossy links, while the dual-priority packet scheduling ensures traffic control is not starved during heavy data transfers.

FLP contributes to the broader field of IoT by demonstrating that commodity hardware, optimized at the software layer, can perform comparably to specialized LPWAN infrastructure at a fraction of the cost. The protocol’s self-healing topology and delay-tolerant design make it suitable for real-world deployment in environmental monitoring, wildfire detection, and biodiversity data collection.

Future works includes: (1) implementing loRa TDMA scheduling to reduce control-plane collisions in dense deployments, (2) field trails in Singapore’s Central Catchment Nature Reserve with 15 - 25 nodes, (3) evaluating ESP-NOW Long Range (LR) mode for extended control signaling coverage within the 2.4 GHz band, and (4) evaluating energy harvesting integration for truly autonomous long-term deployments. (5) adding DTLS support for MQTT-SN to provide end-to-end transport security between mesh nodes and the broker
